# Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Reliable Agents

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is one of the most practical ways to reduce hallucination in LLM agents because it changes where evidence comes from. Instead of forcing the model to rely only on parametric memory, RAG systems retrieve external documents and condition generation on grounded passages. For agents, this matters at two levels: first, retrieval improves factual QA and planning by surfacing missing knowledge; second, retrieved evidence can be cited, checked, and scored, which gives the agent a path to self-correction rather than free-form speculation.

REALM ("Retrieval-Augmented Language Model Pre-Training", Guu et al., 2020) was an early important result showing that retrieval inside the learning loop materially improves knowledge-intensive QA. The model jointly learns a neural retriever and generator over a large corpus. On open-domain QA, REALM reached 40.4 exact match on Natural Questions, 40.7 on WebQuestions, and 46.8 on CuratedTREC. Those numbers were meaningful because they surpassed prior open-book baselines such as ORQA and beat much larger closed-book systems on some settings. For an agent designer, the lesson is that retrieval quality directly affects answer reliability: if the retriever fails to surface the gold evidence, the generator is forced back into guesswork.

The original RAG paper ("Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks", Lewis et al., 2020, arXiv:2005.11401) pushed this further by coupling dense passage retrieval with a seq2seq generator. The paper distinguishes between RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token. RAG-Sequence retrieves passages once and conditions the whole output on them, while RAG-Token allows token-level conditioning that can, in effect, adapt evidence usage across generation steps. On Natural Questions, RAG-Token achieved 44.1 EM and RAG-Sequence achieved 44.5 EM. On TriviaQA the scores were 55.2 and 56.8, on WebQuestions 45.5 and 45.2, and on CuratedTREC 50.0 and 52.2. Those are not "precision" metrics in the IR sense, but they are concrete end-task evidence that better retrieval substantially reduces unsupported answers.

For agents that act over tool calls and documents, the most relevant development is selective or self-reflective RAG rather than fixed retrieval. Self-RAG ("Self-RAG: Learning to Retrieve, Generate, and Critique through Self-Reflection", Asai et al., ICLR 2024) addresses a common production failure mode: blindly retrieving a fixed number of passages can inject irrelevant evidence, distract reasoning, or still fail to support the final claim. Self-RAG trains the model to emit reflection tokens that decide whether retrieval is needed, critique the sufficiency of evidence, and assess whether a generated span is supported. Reported results include 55.8 accuracy on PopQA and 74.5 on TriviaQA for Self-RAG 13B, plus strong factuality gains on long-form generation. Secondary summaries of the paper report citation precision of 70.3 and citation recall of 71.3 on ASQA for Self-RAG 13B. Those numbers matter for agents because citation precision measures whether cited evidence actually supports generated claims, which is closer to operational hallucination control than plain exact match.

In agent settings, RAG reduces hallucination through at least four mechanisms. First, it constrains generation to retrieved evidence rather than unsupported priors. Second, it creates an explicit retrieval interface that can be debugged separately from generation. Third, it allows reranking and confidence scoring to improve precision before synthesis. Fourth, it supports post-hoc auditing, because outputs can be traced back to documents or chunk IDs. This is why many reliable agent designs combine retrieval with verification loops, faithfulness checks, or critic models.

The remaining caveat is that RAG is only as good as its retrieval policy. Low-precision retrieval increases distraction; high recall with poor ranking can swamp the model with irrelevant chunks; and static retrieval can be inefficient or misleading when the task actually needs iterative search. That is why agentic RAG increasingly looks like a control problem rather than a one-shot architecture: retrieve selectively, verify support, and revise when the critic says the evidence is too vague or too broad. REALM, RAG-Token, and Self-RAG are useful milestones in that progression because they show three levels of sophistication: learned retrieval, retrieval-conditioned generation, and retrieval plus self-critique.
